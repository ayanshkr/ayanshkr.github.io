IThe Cosmic Web

Matter in the universe is organized into the Cosmic Web — a large-scale network of filaments, knots, walls, and voids. This structure arose because initial density imbalances were amplified over time: slightly more massive regions attracted slightly less massive ones, causing matter to flow toward filaments and knots.

Simplified Cosmic Web schematic. Project Granite is placed in a filament.
Knots: Too much going on — extreme radiation environments, frequent galaxy mergers, hostile to stable planetary systems.
Voids & Walls: Too little going on — metal-poor environments, sparse star formation, insufficient heavy elements.
✓ Filaments: Our choice. Active star formation, moderate metallicity, and a stable enough environment for long-lived planetary systems. The Local Group (containing the Milky Way) sits in a filament.

IIIThe Problem with Dwarf Galaxies

Dwarf galaxies follow the mass–metallicity relation: smaller galaxies have significantly lower metallicity. This is a problem because metals are the seeds for rocky planet formation.

A typical dwarf galaxy consists of roughly ~95% dark matter, ~3% gas and dust, and only ~2% stars — and the stars that do exist formed in a low-metallicity environment. VIIIHow Are Stars Formed?

Stars form from cold, massive clouds of gas in two main ways:

Compression in Spiral Arms

As gas clouds pass through density waves in the spiral arms, they are compressed. If the compression is sufficient, gravity takes over and the cloud collapses into a star-forming region, often producing clusters of protostars simultaneously.

Bok Globules

Dense, isolated dark nebulae of gas and dust found between spiral arms. Gravity causes them to contract from all directions. They typically produce small groups of stars (2–10) and are one of the few places where star formation can be observed from start to finish.

IXBinary Systems

Binary systems — two stars orbiting a shared centre of mass — are the second most common configuration. They divide into two orbital types:

P-type (Circumbinary) — ~10% of binaries

A planet orbits both stars, which are close together. The two stars can often be treated as a single mass for planetary calculations. If the stars are closer than ~20 million km, they fill their Roche lobes and pull gas from each other.

Minimum stable planet distance from barycentre:

3[(1+e₁)r₁ + (1+e₂)r₂]

r and e relative to barycentre

S-type (Circumstellar) — ~90% of binaries

A planet orbits one star; the second star orbits at a great distance and can largely be ignored. The host star behaves like a unary star within the planet's orbital zone.

[(1−e₁)r₁ + (1−e₂)r₂] / 3

XHigh-Order Systems & Clusters

Trinary Systems (~4%)

Usually hierarchical — e.g. a binary being orbited by a third star at a distance. Rare cases are chaotic and gravitationally indivisible. Planets can exist in stable S-type orbits around individual stars.

Complex Systems (~1%)

Four or more stars. Like trinary systems, usually hierarchical nested configurations. Genuinely chaotic systems do exist but are rare.

Star Clusters

Open clusters: Young, small, loosely bound — disperse over millions of years.
Globular clusters: Ancient, densely packed, tens of thousands to millions of stars. Planetary habitability limited by stellar crowding. Clusters contain ~1/1000 of galaxy's stars but number in the thousands.

XIProject Granite's Star System
Choice: Unary. The most common configuration and the most stable gravitational environment for a developing biosphere. A single star eliminates binary-related complications (tidal distortions from a second star, variable illumination, complex habitable zone geometry), letting us isolate tidal locking as the key variable.

Binary systems are valid worldbuilding choices and sometimes produce more interesting scenarios. This is simply the choice made for this series.

XIIIClass 0: Hypergiants

Hypergiants are the most massive and luminous stars in existence. They are extremely unstable and live only a few hundred thousand years.

  • Begin as Class O (deep blue): >35,000 K, ~25 M☉, ~10 R☉, >100,000 L☉
  • Expand into Class B (blue): ~25,000 K, ~200 R☉
  • Cycle between blue and Class K (orange) phase: ~4,500 K, >1,000 R☉. Each cycle intensifies and shortens as the star sheds mass.
  • Hypernova → metal-rich planetary nebula + black hole remnant
Stars >40 M☉: Stellar winds are so powerful they blow away the outer layers entirely, preventing the red hypergiant transition. At even higher masses the star sheds its entire hydrogen envelope and transitions directly from a main-sequence O-star to a Wolf-Rayet star.
Eliminated: Lifespan of a few hundred thousand years is orders of magnitude too short for complex life. Also produces lethal radiation environments.
XIVClass I: Supergiants

Supergiants are similar to hypergiants but less extreme. Still very short-lived at <1 million years. They start slightly dimmer (>8 M☉, >20,000 K) and follow a similar evolutionary path, cycling between 4,000–10,000 K and 50–400 R☉. Because they have less mass than Class 0, they typically end their lives as neutron stars rather than black holes.

Eliminated: Same fundamental problem as hypergiants — lifespan far too short for complex life.
XV–XVIClass II & III: Giants, and Class IV: Subgiants

Class II — Bright Giants: Expand to 50–100× original size and cool to 3,000–4,000 K. Live longer than supergiants but still not long enough.

Class III — Giants: Expand to 20–50× original size, cooling to 3,000–4,000 K. Spend roughly 10% of their total lifespan in this phase before contracting to white dwarfs. Never live more than ~1 billion years.

Low-mass exception: M-class stars (0.25–0.4 M☉) could theoretically become red giants but this would take longer than the current age of the universe. None have done it yet.

Class IV — Subgiants:

Low mass (0.4–0.9 M☉): Not visually distinct from main sequence, but marked by the formation of a helium core with hydrogen fusion in a shell outside it. Can last hundreds of millions to billions of years.

High mass (>0.9 M☉): A brief transitional phase of contraction and brightening after core hydrogen is exhausted, before outer layers trigger full giant expansion. Lasts only a few million years.

All giant and subgiant classes eliminated: Either too short-lived, or undergoing massive changes in luminosity and size on short geological timescales — both incompatible with the stable irradiation required for complex life.
XVIIGiants Are Bad Company

All Classes 0 through IV are eliminated for the same core reason: they are either extremely short-lived or they undergo massive changes in luminosity and size in a short geological timeframe. Complex life needs billions of years of stable stellar output. None of these stars provide it.

Temperature: >2,300 KMass: >0.08 M☉Radius: >0.1 R☉Luminosity: >0.0003 L☉Lifespan: <10 trillion years% of V: 76%

Most common star type. Immensely long-lived. However, M and some K stars are prone to intense stellar flares that can briefly double their brightness. Below 0.3 M☉ (M3 and smaller): fully convective, no radiative zone, no Red Giant phase — they transition directly to white dwarfs.

XXVIIIHabitability and Tidal Locking

Because planets must orbit very close to K and M stars to remain in the habitable zone, they are usually tidally locked — one face permanently toward the star, one permanently facing away.

This was historically considered a dealbreaker for life. Modern modelling has changed that view:

1. Cloud Formation

Thick clouds form at the substellar point (the hot, wet day side), reflecting sunlight back to space. This increases the planet's albedo and significantly cools the day side, reducing the temperature gradient between hemispheres.

2. Heat Transport

The atmosphere doesn't need to be massive. A surface pressure of just 0.1 atm is sufficient to carry enough heat to the night side to prevent CO₂ from freezing out and the atmosphere from collapsing — even in the complete absence of a magnetic field deflecting the solar wind.

Schematic of a tidally locked planet's temperature distribution.
Project Granite is specifically interested in the effects of tidal locking on complex life development. The K6V star's habitable zone ensures tidal locking while the star itself remains calm enough not to strip the atmosphere.
XXIXInternal Structure and Evolution

F, G, K, and some M stars share a similar internal structure: a radiative zone in the core (energy transported outward by photons) surrounded by a convective zone near the surface (energy transported by bulk movement of plasma). This is the inverse of O/B/A stars.

  • M and some K stars are prone to intense flares that can briefly double their brightness — a significant challenge for atmospheric retention and surface life.
  • Below 0.3 M☉ (M3 and smaller): fully convective throughout with no radiative zone. As a consequence, they have no Red Giant phase — they transition directly from the main sequence to white dwarfs.
  • Above 0.3 M☉: both zones present; will undergo Red Giant expansion before becoming white dwarfs.
XXXYouthful Stages: T-Tauri and Herbig Ae/Be

T-Tauri stars are the young pre-main-sequence phase of F through M stars. They are larger and more active than their mature counterparts — strong stellar winds, intense flaring, significant X-ray and UV output.

This phase lasts approximately 10 million years before nuclear fusion stabilizes. During this period the intense radiation sculpts and photoevaporates the protoplanetary disk, strongly affecting what planets can form and where.

Herbig Ae/Be stars are the corresponding young phase for A and B-type stars — slightly more massive and hotter.

O-type stars evolve so rapidly that by the time they become visible they have already reached the main sequence — no observable pre-main-sequence phase exists for them.

Our K6V star passed through a T-Tauri phase early in its history before settling into the stable state we're working with.

XXXIISubdwarfs — Yerkes Class VI

Cool Subdwarfs (G to M): Smaller and less bright than Population I stars of the same temperature. The reason is lower metallicity, which decreases opacity in the stellar atmosphere.

Lower opacity → less radiation pressure → the star contracts → approximately 2× dimmer than a main sequence star of the same temperature. They are also relatively brighter in UV.

Eliminated: Too little metallicity for rocky planet formation. (Slightly less of a dealbreaker for a captured planet, but that scenario is too unlikely to build a series around.)

Hot Subdwarfs (O and B): Form through different channels.

sdB stars: A binary partner strips off the outer hydrogen layer before enough helium accumulates for helium fusion to begin. These stars skip the giant phase entirely and go straight to white dwarfs.

sdO stars: Also often binary in origin, but more commonly form through the merger of two white dwarfs. Interior: C/O core surrounded by a helium layer with hydrogen at the surface.

Eliminated: Unusual evolutionary history, low metallicity, no stable planetary formation environment.

Mass types by origin:

  • 0.17–0.75 M☉: partner stripped hydrogen off a medium star
  • <0.5 M☉: helium white dwarf from a <0.5 M☉ star (takes longer than the current age of the universe; theoretical)
  • 0.5–1.05 M☉: C-O white dwarf from a 0.5–8 M☉ star
  • 1.05–1.44 M☉: O-Ne-Mg white dwarf from an 8–10 M☉ star

When a white dwarf accretes matter, the accreted layer fuses and produces a bright flash called a nova. If the white dwarf accumulates enough mass to exceed 1.44 M☉ (the Chandrasekhar limit), carbon ignites throughout the entire star — causing a Type Ia Supernova that destroys it completely.

Eliminated: Cool too quickly; would exert massive tidal forces on any habitable-zone planets; the habitable zone migrates rapidly inward, leaving no time for life to adapt.
XXXVHigh-Mass Remnants: Neutron Stars & Black Holes

Neutron Stars form when a stellar remnant between 1.44 and 3 solar masses collapses. Gravity overcomes electron degeneracy pressure, forcing protons and electrons to merge into neutrons and releasing an enormous burst of neutrinos.

  • Radius: ~10 km
  • Density: a teaspoon weighs approximately 10 million tonnes
  • Pulsars: rapidly spinning neutron stars emitting radiation from their magnetic poles — observed as regular pulses from Earth

Black Holes form when the remnant exceeds 3 solar masses. Gravity overcomes all forces holding matter apart — neutrons, then quarks — resulting in complete collapse. Radius scales linearly with mass (minimum ~9 km).

  • Ergosphere: dragged by rotation; impossible to remain stationary
  • Event horizon: point of no return — light cannot escape
  • Ring singularity: matter at a mathematical point (in rotating black holes)
  • Accretion disk: hot infalling matter that can be immensely luminous
Both eliminated: Extreme X-ray and gamma radiation. No stable habitable planetary orbits at any distance compatible with receiving useful warmth.

XXXVIIIWhat is Stellar Metallicity?

Stellar metallicity is the proportion of a star's mass that is neither hydrogen nor helium — i.e., everything heavier (collectively called "metals" in astrophysics, regardless of whether they're chemically metallic).

Why it matters for planets: Metals are the raw material for rocky planet and asteroid formation. Higher metallicity → more material available → higher probability of terrestrial planets.

Why it matters for the star: Metals increase opacity inside the star. More opacity → radiation pressure builds → the star expands slightly and its surface cools. This affects luminosity, temperature, mass loss rate, and ultimately lifespan.

Why it's an age indicator: Only hydrogen, helium, and trace lithium were produced in the Big Bang. Every other element was made inside stars and distributed by supernovae. More metals in a star → it formed from enriched material → it's younger.

XLMeasuring Metallicity in Dex

Metallicity is expressed in dex — the log₁₀ of the metallicity ratio relative to the Sun:

[Fe/H] = log₁₀(Z / Z☉)

Examples:

  • Mu Arae: 0.3 dex because log₁₀(2.4/1.2) = 0.3 (double solar metallicity)
  • C-19 globular cluster: −3.4 dex because log₁₀(0.000478/1.2) = −3.4

Metallicity approximation for the inner thin disc:

[Fe/H] ≈ 0.5 × ln((t − 0.75) / 12)

where t = time since Big Bang in billions of years. Varies ±0.4 dex within the same region.

Note on [Fe/H] vs [M/H]: [Fe/H] measures iron specifically. [M/H] measures total metals. At high metallicity, slowly-burning low-mass stars are more common, relatively enriching C and N. At low metallicity, massive short-lived stars dominated, enriching alpha-process elements (O, Ne, Mg, Si, S).

Thin and thick disc. Age <9 Gyr. Near-circular orbits within the galactic plane. Metal-rich. Our Sun and Project Granite's star are Population I.

Population III

Hypothetical first stars. Pure H and He — no metals at all. Very massive, died fast. None observed; all likely gone billions of years ago.

XLIIEffects of Metallicity on Stellar Evolution

Stellar metallicity significantly affects a star's lifetime, luminosity, radius, mass loss, and temperature. The primary mechanism is opacity:

Radius

More metals → more opaque → radiation pressure increases → star expands. Higher metallicity stars are physically larger at the same mass.

Temperature & Luminosity

Opacity traps heat → surface cools and dims. Higher metallicity → lower surface temperature and luminosity at the same mass.

XLIIIFinal Derivation for Project Granite

Step 1: Current metallicity in the solar neighborhood:

0.5 × ln((13.75 − 0.75) / 12) = +0.04 dex

Step 2: Our system is closer to the galactic center and on the thin/thick disc boundary. This region has about +0.05 dex higher metallicity than the solar neighborhood for stars of the same age — but our star is also older than the Sun, which counteracts this. Net correction applied: −0.05 dex.

Step 3: System is ~6 billion years old (the universe was ~8 Gyr old when it formed):

0.5 × ln((8 − 0.75) / 12) − 0.05 = −0.30 dex
Final metallicity: −0.30 dex
Iron abundance: approximately 0.5× solar (50% of the Sun's iron abundance)
